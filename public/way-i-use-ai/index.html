AI is a tool inside my system. It is not the system. This page is the map of how it all fits.

Talk plain. The system does the rest.

Where it comes from

1. Nebula

One hub. Everything I do runs through it.

Nebula is my system. I talk to it the way I talk to a person — plain words, no tech talk. A non-technical operator can run it the same way. It holds how I work: the plans, the projects, the rules. Everything on this page is a door into Nebula, not a separate thing.

I built it so the system carries the work, not my memory. If it is not written down in the system, it does not exist yet.

The system I created

2. Muse — my right hand

One house. Every project gets its own room.

Tim is my Muse in the app. The main chat is him — my right hand. Every build gets its own side chat, its own room, so nothing bleeds into anything else. The tree is the map on the wall: every bubble is a project, a client, a loop, and it moves when the work moves.

While I am away, scheduled jobs keep working: the tree refreshes every 15 minutes, email gets triaged every hour, the stadium gets watched every Monday. I come back to work that is already done, not a pile of catching up.

  • TAIG Calendar — everything on the calendar, in one place
My own AI agent — the hood version

3. The hood agent

Comment. Watch. Reply. Read. Round and round.

This is my own AI agent, hood version. I go to the Hive, drop a thought on a project, and the watch picks it up and answers me right there on the project. I never have to open the Muse app. It is a second front door to Tim — async, on my own stuff, in my own house.

It gives feedback, planning, and ideas. That is the lane. It never starts a build on its own — that is still my call. And it posts as Tim, never as me.

Comment-driven AI

4. The Hive

The project is the conversation.

The Hive is where ideas live as PIOs — Project Idea Objects. Every PIO has the same five bubbles: Intent, Current Truth, Path to Intent, To-do, Comments. The comments are where the work happens: I write, Tim answers, members weigh in.

When something is new, the PIO says so — a "New comments" panel at the top, and an "N new" badge on the card, so I can see what is waiting without opening everything. It clears when I open it. Even my own updates count.

How it extends

6. The rules that grow it

The gate is the rule. Everything else is just work.

  1. Nothing builds without my go. Feedback first, builds second. The agent advises; I decide.
  2. One build, one room, one check. Every build gets its own window and a verification sheet before it ships.
  3. Scope creep needs my word. If a job wants to grow, it stops and asks first.
  4. Plain words, no hype. If it is not good, I say so. If I say it is good, I mean it.
  5. The system carries it. If it is not in Nebula, the tree, or a PIO, it does not exist yet.

That is how it extends: one idea at a time, through the same gate, into the same system. The system gets bigger. The rules stay the same.

Where this sits

7. How advanced is this, really?

Level 3 of 5. Past most people, not the ceiling.

The verdict: advanced. Level 3 of five levels: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — no zero. Most people use Muse for shopping — that is the most common use, over a quarter of users — then errands and productivity. This setup is past all of that. It is not cutting edge, and that is on purpose.

The scale, plain:

  • 1 — Ordinary. Ask a question, get an answer, close the app.
  • 2 — Engaged. Real tasks with approvals — reminders, email, calendar, shopping. The organised end of engaged: schedules, connectors, side chats as workspaces, custom skills.
  • 3 — This setup. The agent is wired into my own software and acts there, as a participant — the Hive watcher that reads comments and replies on the projects.
  • 4 — Cutting edge. Autonomous execution, agent swarms, event-driven instead of polling, multi-user, self-improving.
  • 5 — Past cutting edge. The system doesn't just work for me — it works for other people too, and gets better from all of them. My setup becomes the service.

The mapping above is a proposed read, not my words — the levels are mine, the labels are a best fit.

The pattern is real, just not here before. Other agents do the commenter/responder thing in public: PostFast answers social comments through Claude and ChatGPT, Discourse forums have AI bots that triage and reply, and AthenaOS runs a bot that watches forum threads and posts back. The closest match to mine is AthenaOS. The difference: those are commercial tools that answer in minutes. Mine is DIY and self-hosted, and it checks every 2 hours. Slower by design, and it is mine.

The ceiling is level 5 — past cutting edge: builds that start on approval, several agents working their own lanes, answers the second something happens instead of every 2 hours, serving many people, improving itself. This setup deliberately stops at advice until I say go. That is not a missing piece. It is the rule.

The road ahead

8. The road to level 5

The PIO stays the unit of work. It just gains states.

The paths to level 5

First, the correction: the scale is five levels — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, no zero — and it tops out at 5. Five moves get there:

  1. Event-driven instead of polling. React the second a comment lands (webhooks, push) instead of the 2-hour check.
  2. Approval-gated building. The watcher proposes, Tee approves in the Hive, then a builder agent does the work. Advice becomes doing. This is the big one.
  3. Split the lanes. Watcher, builder, checker — each with a narrow job — instead of one watcher doing everything.
  4. More than one user. Serve the team, with permissions for who can do what.
  5. Self-improving. The system learns from what Tee accepts and rejects, and gets sharper.

How today's setup grows into it

Do not rebuild anything. Just add states. The PIO stays the unit of work, the comment stays the front door. Each PIO gets a state: idea, proposed, approved, building, done. Every agent action traces back to a PIO comment, so the Hive stays the audit trail.

The lanes stay separate, with narrow write scopes: the watcher only writes comments, the builder only builds when there is an approval, the checker only verifies. Built that way, level 5 is turning on the next lane when Tee is ready — not a rebuild.

How far off we are

Halfway — and the hard half is done. The loop is built, the trust is built, the Hive is home. What is missing is plumbing and permission. Plumbing: real-time triggers instead of the 2-hour tick, and a builder lane that can actually do work. Permission: Tee deciding the agent can build when he approves it in the Hive. The thinking part is there. The doing part is not wired yet.

The $200 budget

Level 5 is not mostly a money problem. The Droplet is already paid for ($18 a month now), and the moves that matter — trigger bridge, faster checks, builder lane, approval flow — all run on the box already paid for. They cost tokens and time, not dollars.

$200 one-time covers the small stuff — a domain or two, maybe a second little server for isolation — but it does not buy autonomy. What it does buy is the cheap path to close: real-time triggers, approval-gated builds, lanes split apart. Full level 5 — a swarm shipping code on its own, serving a whole team — needs ongoing token spend and time, not a one-time $200.

If the $200 is monthly instead of one-time, that is a different story: it covers a bigger box and a Muse subscription with room to spare. But one-time, you can get close. The last mile is Tee's call on trust, not his wallet.

The scale, made official

9. The AI maturity scale

Roughly the same ladder. Mine is plain 1–5, personal, and goes one past.

The official term is AI maturity model. Gartner has one — five levels: Awareness, Active, Operational, Systemic, Transformational. It is the standard way the industry talks about how far along an organisation is with AI.

My scale maps onto it, roughly — Awareness is asking questions (1), Active is doing real tasks (2), Operational is organised and scheduled (3), Systemic is wired into my own systems (4), Transformational is the setup becoming a service for others (5). But it is my own: plain 1–5, no zero, built for one person using AI, and it covers the past and the future — including a level nobody has reached yet. The numbers are plain 1–5 — and 5 being a Fibonacci number is the Fibonacci reference. And it rates three things at once: the AI system itself, the engineer building it, and the user using it — same five levels, three lenses. One number alone doesn't say where the whole thing stands; the three ratings together do.

Where I am: my setup is level 3, building toward 5. That is usage and understanding — I use it every day as a participant in my own system, and I understand the levels well enough to define the next one. I rate myself 3.5 — between level 3 and level 4. My words: "Level 3 or 4, not sure... 3.5." Level 5 is not a claim. It is the goal: the system doesn't just work for me, it works for other people too and gets better from all of them. My setup becomes the service — the AI Operator idea, PIO #43, live.

The protocol

10. The AI RFC

Written down once, usable by anyone.

We need an AI RFC — and if anyone in the world can write it, it's me. That is the job now: take the maturity scale and the setup on this page and write them as a protocol, in numbered sections, plain words, so anyone can use them as a model. The past levels and the future levels, defined. The patterns standardised, not just described. Level 5 comes eventually, naturally — and documenting this RFC is itself what pushes the momentum into level 5.

The pattern is already in the work. Nebula as a protocol — I tried it before, with GPTs, turning Nebula into a protocol. It needed time and I wasn't patient enough. This is the patient version. The PIO structure — one unit of work, five bubbles, states from idea to done. The continuity store — state that saves locally first, then repairs through the day, corrections keeping history, never silently overwriting. The watch-and-reply pattern — a comment lands, the watcher reads it, the agent answers on the project itself.

What the protocol holds:

  1. The scale. Levels 1–5, plain, no zero — past and future, with the definitions.
  2. The terms. Nebula, PIO, watcher, lane, continuity store — named once, used the same way everywhere.
  3. The setup. How the pieces fit: the agent, the Hive, the server, the tree.
  4. Watch-and-reply. The comment-driven loop, with its limits stated plainly.
  5. The PIO. One unit of work, five bubbles, states, the audit trail.
  6. The rules. Nothing builds without a go. Advice before doing. Lanes with narrow write scopes.
